Attack 1

Show that any countable subset of \(\mathbb{R}\) has Lebesgue outer measure zero.

Timer not started. Twenty minutes of real work before a solution.

Enumerate the set as \(x_n\). Cover \(x_n\) by an interval of length \(\varepsilon/2^n\). The total length is \(\varepsilon\). Outer measure is the infimum of such covers, so it is 0.

What this actually is

Measure theory replaces the Riemann integral, which cannot handle a pointwise limit of functions without an extra uniform-convergence hypothesis you often do not have. A measure assigns sizes to a σ-algebra of sets. Lebesgue measure on \(\mathbb{R}\) extends length. The integral is built from simple functions up, and the convergence theorems — monotone convergence, Fatou, dominated convergence — are the reason the subject exists. \(L^p\) spaces are complete. That completeness is what makes Fourier analysis and probability sit on the same foundation.

Sheldon Axler’s Measure, Integration & Real Analysis is free from the author and is the main text. Rudin’s Real and Complex Analysis is the paid ceiling; no ISBN was verified for that specific title in this pass, so none is printed. Folland is the other standard paid book, same situation. You do not need either to learn the convergence theorems. You need to prove them.

A set of measure zero is not a set you can ignore inside a definition. It is a set you can ignore inside a statement that has already been proved to depend only on almost-everywhere behavior.

Core results

Statements only. Write the argument before you reveal it. A revealed proof you did not attempt is a paragraph you will not be able to rebuild at 2am.

  1. Measures. A σ-algebra is closed under complements and countable unions. A measure is a countably additive \([0,\infty]\)-valued function on it, with \(\mu(\emptyset)=0\). Lebesgue measure is the completion of the restriction of outer measure to the Carathéodory-measurable sets, and it extends length on intervals.

    Countable additivity for disjoint intervals is a standard estimate. Carathéodory’s criterion produces a σ-algebra on which outer measure is a measure. Completion adds subsets of null sets.

  2. Integral. The integral of a nonnegative simple function is the obvious sum. For a nonnegative measurable function it is the supremum of integrals of simple functions underneath. For a general measurable function it is the difference of the positive and negative parts, when at least one is finite.

    Well-definedness of the simple-function integral uses a common refinement of the level sets. Monotone convergence is then used to prove linearity for nonnegative functions. Write that once.

  3. Convergence theorems. Monotone convergence: if \(0\le f_n\uparrow f\) then the integrals rise to the integral of \(f\). Fatou: the integral of the liminf is at most the liminf of the integrals. Dominated convergence is the statement in the attack.

    Monotone convergence is the technical heart; Fatou is monotone convergence applied to the infima from each index onward; dominated convergence is Fatou applied to \(g+f_n\) and \(g-f_n\).

  4. Fubini. For σ-finite measures, the integral of a nonnegative measurable function on a product equals the iterated integrals in either order. For integrable functions of either sign, the same holds, and almost every slice is integrable.

    Prove it for indicators of measurable rectangles, then for simple functions, then by monotone convergence. The signed case uses integrability to justify the difference.

  5. L^p. \(L^p\) is the space of measurable functions with \(\int |f|^p<\infty\), modulo almost-everywhere equality. It is a Banach space for \(1\le p\le\infty\). Hölder’s inequality is the tool that makes the dual of \(L^p\) look like \(L^q\).

    Completeness is: a fast Cauchy sequence has a subsequence with \(\|f_{n_{k+1}}-f_{n_k}\|<2^{-k}\), the telescoping series converges absolutely almost everywhere by monotone convergence, and the limit is the \(L^p\) limit. Hölder is Young’s inequality integrated.

Hardest questions

Names and links. The problem text stays on the source site. Copyrighted contest problems are not copied here. What follows is where they are and why they hurt.

  1. Axler, monotone convergence and the exercises after it
    Every later theorem is this one with a costume.
  2. A counterexample to interchanging limit and integral
    The spikes \(n\mathbf{1}_{(0,1/n)}\) are the prototype. You need two more, including one where Fatou is strict.
  3. Prove L^p is complete
    The subsequence trick is the one you will reuse for the rest of your life.
  4. Fubini for a nonnegative function, written
    People apply Fubini to signed functions they have not shown to be integrable. The proof tells you why the hypothesis is there.
  5. State Hölder and use it to prove the dual pairing of L^p and L^q is well-defined
    This is the door to functional analysis. A slogan does not open it.

Mastery gate

Pass: from memory, the proofs of monotone convergence’s consequences (Fatou and dominated convergence, assuming monotone convergence) and the construction of a non-integrable pointwise limit of integrable functions. Plus the completeness of \(L^1\). Fail if “almost everywhere” is not in the statements.

Diagrams

Escalating sets

Five sets. Finish a set before you touch the next. Full sentences, not a circled answer.

Set 1. Measure

  1. Outer measure of a countable set.
  2. Prove countable subadditivity of outer measure.
  3. Show a single point has measure zero.
  4. Why is the Vitali set nonmeasurable? Outline, including where choice is used.
  5. Intervals are measurable. Say which theorem you are quoting.

Set 2. Functions

  1. A function is measurable iff preimages of intervals \((-\infty,a)\) are measurable. Prove one direction.
  2. Pointwise limits of measurable functions are measurable.
  3. Simple-function approximation of a nonnegative measurable function.
  4. Integral of a simple function, well-defined.
  5. \(\int n 1_{(0,1/n)}=1\).

Set 3. Theorems

  1. State monotone convergence.
  2. Deduce Fatou.
  3. Deduce dominated convergence.
  4. Give a case where Fatou is strict.
  5. Give a case where there is no integrable dominant.

Set 4. Product and L^p

  1. Fubini’s hypothesis for signed functions.
  2. Hölder for sums, then for integrals.
  3. Prove L^1 completeness with the subsequence trick.
  4. \(\|f\|_\infty\) as an essential supremum. Give a function where the essential supremum is smaller than every bound you would guess from pointwise values on the rationals... or explain why that cannot happen for a continuous function.
  5. Minkowski’s inequality, stated.

Set 5. Ceiling

  1. All three convergence theorems, memory.
  2. Completeness of L^p.
  3. One counterexample page, three examples.
  4. Fubini outline.
  5. Oral definition of the integral.
