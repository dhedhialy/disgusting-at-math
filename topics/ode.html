Problems first

Attempt these before you open a book or a video. Write the argument in the work box and the conclusion in the answer box. Check counts the problem only when both pass, and it does not open the solution. The solution stays locked for twenty minutes after you start the timer.

Attack 1

Solve \(y'=y\) with \(y(0)=1\).

Timer not started. Twenty minutes of real work before a solution.

Separable: \(dy/y=dt\), so \(\ln|y|=t+C\), hence \(y=Ae^{t}\). The condition \(y(0)=1\) gives \(A=1\). Or recognize that the derivative equaling the function characterizes the exponential.

Attack 2

Solve \(y'+y=0\).

Timer not started. Twenty minutes of real work before a solution.

\(y=Ce^{-t}\). Integrating factor \(e^{t}\) turns the left side into \((ye^{t})'\).

Attack 3

For \(y'=y(1-y)\), find the equilibria and say which are stable.

Timer not started. Twenty minutes of real work before a solution.

Equilibria at \(y=0\) and \(y=1\). For \(y\in(0,1)\) the right-hand side is positive, so arrows point up toward 1 and away from 0. For \(y>1\) it is negative, toward 1. For \(y<0\) it is negative, away from 0. So 1 is stable and 0 is unstable.

What this actually is

An ordinary differential equation asks for a function whose derivatives satisfy a relation. The first course has two layers. The computational layer is a list of solvable classes: separable equations, first-order linear equations via an integrating factor, second-order linear equations with constant coefficients, Laplace transforms, and power-series solutions at ordinary points. The structural layer is what you keep: existence and uniqueness, the phase line and the phase plane, linearization near equilibria, and the fact that an \(n\)th-order equation is a first-order system in a higher-dimensional space.

Arnold’s Ordinary Differential Equations is the ceiling. The MIT Press edition has ISBN 978-0-262-51018-9. It is paid, and it is the book whose problems are worth more than another technique chapter. Boyce and DiPrima is the standard paid course text; no edition was verified, so none is cited. Tenenbaum and Pollard, ISBN 978-0-486-64940-5, is the huge Dover problem collection. You will actually work MIT 18.03, which has the lectures, recitations, and exams.

A phase portrait you cannot sketch from the equation is a picture you copied. Sketch before you solve.

Core results

Statements only. Write the argument before you reveal it. A revealed proof you did not attempt is a paragraph you will not be able to rebuild at 2am.

  1. First order. Separable: \(y'=f(x)g(y)\). Linear: \(y'+p(x)y=q(x)\) has integrating factor \(e^{\int p}\). Picard–Lindelöf: if \(f(t,y)\) is continuous and Lipschitz in \(y\) near \((t_0,y_0)\), the initial-value problem has a unique solution on some interval.

    The integrating factor is chosen so the left side becomes a derivative. Picard iteration is the contraction mapping on a short-time space of continuous functions; you should be able to state the theorem now and prove the contraction estimate when you have analysis under your belt. Do not pretend the theorem is “just separation of variables.”

  2. Second-order linear. For \(y''+ay'+by=0\), try \(e^{rt}\) and solve the characteristic polynomial. Repeated roots bring a factor of \(t\). A particular solution of the nonhomogeneous equation plus the homogeneous general solution is the general solution. Wronskian nonzero means the two homogeneous solutions form a basis.

    The equation is linear, so the solution space of the homogeneous problem is a vector space; the existence theorem says the dimension equals the order. The characteristic polynomial is the computation. Variation of parameters produces a particular solution from the Wronskian.

  3. Systems and phase planes. \(\mathbf x'=A\mathbf x\) is solved by the exponential \(e^{tA}\), computed via Jordan form or, when \(A\) is diagonalizable, via eigenvalues. Equilibria of \(\mathbf x'=\mathbf F(\mathbf x)\) are where \(\mathbf F=0\). The linearization \(D\mathbf F\) classifies a hyperbolic equilibrium. A sign chart or nullclines sketch the rest.

    If \(Av=\lambda v\) then \(e^{\lambda t}v\) solves the system. The linearization theorem says a hyperbolic equilibrium looks like its linear part; the proof is Hartman–Grobman, which you may quote after you have checked the eigenvalues are off the imaginary axis.

  4. Laplace transforms. \(\mathcal L\{y\}(s)=\int_0^\infty e^{-st}y(t)\,dt\). It turns \(y'\) into \(sY-y(0)\) and convolutions into products, under growth hypotheses that justify the integrals.

    Integration by parts on the integral for \(y'\), with the boundary term at infinity killed by the exponential decay you assumed. Tables are not a proof. Derive the transforms of \(1\), \(e^{at}\), and \(\sin bt\) once.

  5. Series and a first PDE. At an ordinary point, a linear ODE has a power-series solution whose radius is at least the distance to the nearest complex singularity of the coefficients. The heat equation \(u_t=u_{xx}\) and the wave equation \(u_{tt}=c^2 u_{xx}\), with boundary conditions, are solved by separating variables into an ODE eigenvalue problem.

    The series method is plugging in \(\sum a_n x^n\) and matching coefficients; convergence needs a majorant or a citation of the existence theorem in the complex domain. Separation of variables is linear algebra: eigenfunctions of a symmetric differential operator form a basis you can expand the initial data in. You will prove the basis claim in a later course; here, do it for sine series on \([0,\pi]\) by quoting Fourier.

The ladder

Four rungs, in order. Do not skip a rung because the next one looks more serious.

Tier 0 / First contact

Watch or read once. Do not study from this.

Hardest questions

Names and links. The problem text stays on the source site. Copyrighted contest problems are not copied here. What follows is where they are and why they hurt.

  1. MIT 18.03 exams
    Phase portraits and linear systems under time. The algebra is short. The picture is the answer.
  2. Arnold, the problem sections on phase portraits and linearization
    Arnold is paid and unlinked as a file. The Putnam archive stands in as timed problems that use the same taste: qualitative, not formulaic. Buy Arnold if you can.
  3. 18.03 problem sets on linear systems
    Drawing nullclines and deciding stability from a matrix is the skill that transfers to dynamics.
  4. A series solution at an ordinary point, from the 18.03 sets
    The recurrence is easy to mis-index. That is the whole difficulty.
  5. Separation of variables for the heat equation on an interval, one 18.03 or later problem
    You have to keep the boundary conditions in the eigenvalue problem. Drop them and the series is fiction.

Mastery gate

Pass: one 18.03 exam, timed, including a phase-plane problem drawn without a computer, plus a written statement of Picard–Lindelöf with the Lipschitz hypothesis actually present. Fail if “stable” means “I solved it and the formula looks small.”

Diagrams

Escalating sets

Five sets. Finish a set before you touch the next. Full sentences, not a circled answer.

Set 1. First order

  1. Solve \(y'=y\), \(y(0)=1\).
  2. Solve \(y'+y=e^t\).
  3. Solve \(y'=y(1-y)\) by separating, and compare with the phase line.
  4. Where does \(y'=\sqrt{y}\), \(y(0)=0\), fail uniqueness? Exhibit two solutions.
  5. State Picard–Lindelöf.

Set 2. Second order

  1. Solve \(y''+y=0\), \(y(0)=1\), \(y'(0)=0\).
  2. Solve \(y''-3y'+2y=e^{t}\).
  3. Repeated root: \(y''-2y'+y=0\).
  4. Write the Wronskian of \(\cos t\) and \(\sin t\) and say what it tells you.
  5. Variation of parameters for \(y''+y=\sec t\) on \((-\pi/2,\pi/2)\).

Set 3. Systems

  1. Solve \(\mathbf x'=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}\mathbf x\).
  2. Classify the origin for a spiral, a node, a saddle, from the eigenvalues. Draw one of each.
  3. Linearize \(x'=x-y\), \(y'=x+y-x^3\) is optional; do \(x'=-y\), \(y'=x-y\) and classify.
  4. Nullclines of \(x'=x(1-x-y)\), \(y'=y(1-2x)\). Equilibria only, then a sketch.
  5. Convert \(y''+y'+y=0\) into a first-order system.

Set 4. Transforms and series

  1. Laplace transform of \(e^{at}\) from the integral.
  2. Solve \(y''+y=0\), \(y(0)=1\), \(y'(0)=0\) by Laplace transforms.
  3. Series solution of \(y''+xy=0\) at 0, first four nonzero terms, and the recurrence.
  4. Separate variables: \(u_t=u_{xx}\), \(u(0,t)=u(\pi,t)=0\), \(u(x,0)=\sin x\).
  5. Why does a repeated Laplace calculation without growth hypotheses not count as a proof?

Set 5. Ceiling

  1. One 18.03 exam.
  2. Three phase portraits from scratch.
  3. Picard–Lindelöf, hypotheses written from memory.
  4. One Arnold problem if you have the book; otherwise one extra 18.03 exam.
  5. The heat equation example, including why the eigenvalue is what it is.
