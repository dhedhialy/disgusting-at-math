Problems first

Attempt these before you open a book or a video. Write the argument in the work box and the conclusion in the answer box. Check counts the problem only when both pass, and it does not open the solution. The solution stays locked for twenty minutes after you start the timer.

Attack 1

Write the Cauchy–Riemann equations for \(f=u+iv\), and check them for \(f(z)=z^2\).

Timer not started. Twenty minutes of real work before a solution.

\(u_x=v_y\) and \(u_y=-v_x\). For \(z^2=(x+iy)^2=x^2-y^2+2ixy\), one has \(u_x=2x=v_y\) and \(u_y=-2y=-v_x\).

Attack 2

Compute \(\int_{|z|=1} dz/z\), counterclockwise.

Timer not started. Twenty minutes of real work before a solution.

Parametrize \(z=e^{i\theta}\), \(dz=ie^{i\theta}d\theta\), \(\theta\) from 0 to \(2\pi\). The integral is \(\int_0^{2\pi} i\,d\theta=2\pi i\).

Attack 3

Why does Cauchy–Riemann for \(f(z)=\bar z\) fail?

Timer not started. Twenty minutes of real work before a solution.

\(u=x\), \(v=-y\). Then \(u_x=1\) and \(v_y=-1\), so \(u_x\ne v_y\). Conjugation is not holomorphic anywhere.

What this actually is

Complex analysis is calculus for functions \(\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}\) that are complex-differentiable on an open set. That single hypothesis is absurdly strong. It implies the function is analytic, that its integral over a triangle vanishes, that Cauchy's integral formula recovers the function from its values on a circle, and that a bounded entire function is constant. Residues then turn real integrals into contour bookkeeping. You are not learning a new computational trick. You are learning why holomorphicity is a rigidity condition.

MIT 18.04 is a full course with notes and problem sets, and it is the working text because a standard book such as Ahlfors or Conway is paid and no free legal scan was verified. Stein and Shakarchi, and Needham’s Visual Complex Analysis, are paid and unnamed by ISBN here for the same reason. Use 18.04’s problem sets as the book. The proofs you must own are Cauchy's theorem for a disk, the integral formula, and the residue theorem at the level of a simple pole.

A formal power series you cannot connect to an integral is not yet complex analysis.

Core results

Statements only. Write the argument before you reveal it. A revealed proof you did not attempt is a paragraph you will not be able to rebuild at 2am.

  1. Holomorphic. \(f\) is holomorphic at \(z_0\) when \(\lim_{h\to 0}(f(z_0+h)-f(z_0))/h\) exists in \(\mathbb{C}\). On an open set this is equivalent, given continuous partials, to the Cauchy–Riemann equations.

    Approach along real and imaginary directions to get Cauchy–Riemann. The converse, with continuous partials, writes the real differential and matches it to multiplication by a complex number.

  2. Cauchy. If \(f\) is holomorphic inside and on a triangle, the integral over the boundary is 0. Cauchy's integral formula: \(f(a)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_\gamma f(z)/(z-a)\,dz\) for \(a\) inside a suitable closed curve.

    Goursat’s proof for a triangle subdivides and uses a uniform estimate on the difference quotient. The integral formula reduces to the integral of \(1/(z-a)\) by writing \(f(z)=f(a)+(f(z)-f(a))\) and showing the second term integrates to 0.

  3. Consequences. Holomorphic functions are analytic. Liouville: a bounded entire function is constant. The identity theorem: if two holomorphic functions on a connected open set agree on a set with a limit point, they agree everywhere.

    Expand via Cauchy's formula as a power series. Liouville: Cauchy's estimates force every Taylor coefficient past the constant term to vanish when the function is bounded. The identity theorem uses isolation of zeros of a non identically zero holomorphic function.

  4. Residues. The residue at an isolated singularity is the coefficient of \(1/(z-a)\) in the Laurent series, and the integral over a small circle is \(2\pi i\) times that coefficient. A simple pole of \(p/q\) at a simple zero of \(q\) has residue \(p(a)/q'(a)\).

    Laurent coefficients are Cauchy integrals. Only the \(1/(z-a)\) term survives integration over a circle. The simple-pole formula is the limit \((z-a)p(z)/q(z)\) as \(z\to a\), which is \(p(a)/q'(a)\) by differentiability of \(q\).

  5. Real integrals. A standard real integral of a rational function of sine and cosine, or a rational function with enough decay, can be read off from residues of a related complex function, provided you justify the vanishing of the integral on the closing arc.

    The justification is an estimate: length of the arc times the maximum of the modulus. Write one such estimate, for a semicircle and a function with degree of the denominator at least two more than the numerator, before you use a table.

Names and links. The problem text stays on the source site. Copyrighted contest problems are not copied here. What follows is where they are and why they hurt.

    You have to say where the function is holomorphic, not just compute.

  3. Compute a real improper integral by residues, with the arc estimate written
    The estimate is the part everyone skips and the part the grader wants.

Mastery gate

Pass: a timed 18.04 exam, plus written proofs of Cauchy's integral formula for a circle and of Liouville’s theorem. Fail if a residue computation has no sentence about the contour.

Diagrams

Escalating sets

Five sets. Finish a set before you touch the next. Full sentences, not a circled answer.

Set 1. Differentiability

  1. Cauchy–Riemann for \(z^2\) and for \(\bar z\).
  2. Prove that a holomorphic function with zero derivative is locally constant, on a disk.
  3. Show \(e^z\) is entire.
  4. Find where \(|z|^2\) is complex differentiable.
  5. Express \(f'(z)\) in terms of \(u_x\) and \(v_x\).

Set 2. Integrals

  1. \(\int_{|z|=1} dz/z\).
  2. \(\int_{|z|=2} dz/(z-1)\).
  3. State Goursat’s theorem.
  4. Prove the integral of an exact derivative \(F'\) over a closed curve is zero when \(F\) is entire.
  5. Estimate \(|\int_\gamma f|\le \mathrm{length}(\gamma)\cdot\max|f|\).

Set 3. Consequences

  1. Derive the Taylor series of \(1/(1-z)\) on the unit disk.
  2. Prove Liouville.
  3. Prove the fundamental theorem of algebra from Liouville.
  4. State the identity theorem.
  5. Give two different holomorphic functions that agree on \(\{1/n\}\). Explain why this does not contradict the identity theorem.

Set 4. Residues

  1. Residue of \(1/(z^2+1)\) at \(i\).
  2. Simple-pole formula, proved.
  3. A semicircle estimate for \(1/(z^2+1)\).
  4. Compute \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} dx/(x^2+1)\) by residues.
  5. A function with an essential singularity: \(e^{1/z}\). Name one Laurent coefficient.

Set 5. Ceiling

  1. One 18.04 exam.
  2. Liouville from memory.
  3. One branch-cut problem from the course.
  4. Fundamental theorem of algebra via Liouville.
  5. Oral statement of Cauchy's formula.
